## Post 2: We publish our failures (week 2)

Image: none, or a simple text card (to make later).

```
In October we ran 42 tests on our drug-ranking engine.

39 of them failed.

For each one, we wrote down the change and the rule it had to pass, committed that, and only then ran the test. When a change missed its rule, even narrowly, we did not adopt it, and we published the result anyway.

Why work this way? Because in rare-disease research it is easy to find a pattern you were hoping for. Declaring the test first is how we keep ourselves honest, and publishing the failures is how others can check us.

Every link in the atlas works the same way: it shows its source, and every quote is checked word for word against it. Content extracted by AI is labelled as AI.

How we test, and every result, including the bad ones: phenotech.mehro.ch

#RareDisease #ReproducibleResearch #OpenScience
```

## Post 3: What a forward test shows, and what it doesn't (week 3)

```
Can an algorithm point research towards existing drugs for rare diseases before anyone tries them?

We tested it this way. We rebuilt our ranking using only data from before 2021, then looked at what happened afterwards. Of 983 new uses of drugs in rare diseases registered since, 35.0% were already in that drug's top 20, out of 8,723 diseases. Picking at random would give 0.2%. From 2023, the figure is 37.2% of 561.

What this does not show:
• A "use" means a registered trial, so the test measures where drugs get tried, not whether they work.
• The engine is weaker for the least-studied diseases, which are the ones that need it most.
• No independent expert has reviewed the suggestions yet. Our frozen forecasts will be scored in April and October 2027.

So we call them research leads. The full method and every figure: phenotech.mehro.ch

#DrugRepurposing #RareDisease #MachineLearning
```
